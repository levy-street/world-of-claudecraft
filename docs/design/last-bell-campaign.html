At a glance

Campaign roughly 3 to 4 hours; optional Willowfen epilogue 20 to 30 minutes; separate repeatable five-player aftermath dungeon. Solo or a party of up to five. Completion rewards memorialize, never pay out grief: a title, the squad's banner, Renown, and Tam's bell-striker as a keepsake; gear progression comes from the dungeon. The pillars: 1. The story is the reward. 2. The player should genuinely cry. 3. The squad fights beside you. 4. Everything new is reusable.

The promise (full spoilers, by design)

Gullhaven can be saved for another century, but the people who seal its great breach will not come home. Nobody knows that, including them. The plan is to walk in, set the charge, and walk out together: Ollun believes the Bellheart and Edda's charges have engineered past the old price on Hale's statue. Then the old price returns (the ward must be held by a living hand until the fire: one stays), and then the wound answers (it divides into four wards and kills the fuse: five stay). The player is forced out because the seal needs one final hand on the outside.

Emotional guardrails (binding, full spoilers)
  • The squad enters the breach expecting to walk out together: the inside plan genuinely engineers past Hale's price, and Ollun believes it. Twist one restores the old price (the ward must be held: one). Twist two raises it (four wards and a dead fuse: five).
  • Both twists land mechanically and visibly, never as narration: the player watches the ward drift when hands leave it, and watches the fuse die.
  • The campaign baits one false last stand (the breakwater stair in Q6) and gives him back; it never fakes the real sacrifice afterward.
  • The interior precedent is public knowledge (Warden Hale). Coalfast's private certainty is only that the carrier will be him.
  • The reinforcements die because of a defensible collective decision (the vote) followed by a defensible battlefield call (Edda's flare).
  • The player contributes to the victory: they kill enemies, carry the Bellheart out, and physically close the breach.
  • Nobody is secretly alive afterward. Choices color the story; they never branch it; no choice can avert the sacrifice.
  • The squad reads as elite at all times. Their fear measures the enemy's scale, never their own frailty.
  • The ending is allowed to remain quiet.

The quick read

The world

  • There are two worlds: ours (solid, finished) and the Sleeping World (a nightmare place, never finished).
  • A barrier called the Loom kept them apart. It shattered: the Night of Glass. The sky over the Farshore never healed.
  • Rifts are nightmares leaking through the cuts. Small ones open constantly (the island's weather); a huge one, the great breach, opens about once a century.
  • Star-glass is fallen pieces of the broken barrier, and the only material rifts respond to: it can hear them, seal them, or burn them.
  • The Vigil is the order that seals rifts. Its method, the rite, is old, boring, and reliable: star-glass wards placed and tuned around a wound until it closes.

The island

  • Gullhaven has kept this watch for 1,200 years, forgotten by its parent order.
  • Its founders' treasure, the Bellheart, vibrates before a rift opens. The island's whole bell-warning code grew from it.
  • Generations ago the bell tower collapsed into the sea and took the Bellheart with it.
  • 100 years ago the great breach opened, the rite failed without the Bellheart, and Warden Hale carried the seal inside alone. He never came out. Nobody knows why the ones who go in stay.
  • Now the breach is due again, and every sign says this one is the worst ever recorded.

The people

  • You: a newcomer who proves too capable to ignore.
  • Coalfast: the commander. Decided long ago, privately, that if someone must go in, it is him.
  • Ollun: the scholar. Reads breaches the way a physician reads a chest. Never talks about his own future.
  • Edda: the engineer. Builds the charges, names her machines, jokes hardest when scared.
  • Saul: the healer. Treats every patient by name.
  • Tam: the bellkeeper. Claims the rites are nonsense, performs them perfectly. His silence is the alarm.
  • Bren: the squad's scout. Died weeks before you arrive; his chair stays empty. His daughter Nell runs the bell messages.

The story, beat by beat

  1. You arrive, the bell rings, and you kill something the militia could not. The squad, short a scout, recruits you.
  2. One night of patrol breaks twelve centuries of rift records. Something very big is coming.
  3. The squad raises the Bellheart from the drowned tower. It comes up cracked: it can still hear rifts, but it cannot ring.
  4. The council votes (you included) to call the mainland for relief: extra hands, so the town can be evacuated while the seal is raised.
  5. The breach surges early and the fleet is called in at night. A creature called the Riftjaw is waiting. The fleet dies in full view, and the Riftjaw swallows the sailors' souls. The wound feeds on them.
  6. The squad salvages the dead fleet's star-glass: it builds the seal's wards, and at Edda's forge it rebuilds the Bellheart's voice. Reforged, the bell speaks at full strength for the first time in generations.
  7. The outer seal is raised by the book and nearly works, then fails: this wound's heart is not in our world, so it cannot be closed from outside. Exactly what happened in Hale's year.
  8. New plan: carry the seal inside. Set charges at the heart, hold the wound shut, burn the heart out, walk out together, and lock the scar from outside with the Bellheart. Everyone believes nobody has to die this time.
  9. Inside, they learn the truth about Hale: the ward will not hold unless a living mind keeps holding it, until the fire. Someone has to stay. Coalfast takes it without a word.
  10. The wound fights back: it splits the ward into four and kills the fuse. It has learned the rite, and the fleet's souls made it strong enough to answer it. Five stations, one for each of them. Nobody hesitates.
  11. You are ordered out, because the lock must be turned from outside and only you are left to turn it. The charge fires; you strike the Bellheart; the breach closes. The five do not come home. Gullhaven is safe for another century.
  12. Afterward: you close the watch alone, Nell takes over the bell, and the Bellheart goes to the parent order, so the next great breach, anywhere, will be heard coming.

Still open

  • The wounded Riftjaw survives beneath the wreck fields, still holding the swallowed souls: the post-campaign dungeon. Killing it frees them; it resurrects no one.
  • Something on the far side learned the rite. That thread is left for a future arc.

The story

The campaign start to finish, independent of any quest. This is the source material: the beat sheets, dialogue, and scenes are this story cut into playable pieces, and when they disagree, this page wins. It is written in experience order, so read it like a book; the spoiled summary lives on the Overview tab, marked.

The Night of Glass

There are two worlds. Ours is the waking world, and it is finished: solid, settled, it stays the way you leave it. The other is the Sleeping World, and it is not a gentle place. It is where the Dreamer sleeps, and at the shallow edge of that sleep the nightmares form: rooms with no way out, tides that pull without water, half-made things hungry to be finished. Between the two worlds hung the Loom, a barrier woven before any record began. It kept the nightmares where they belong.

On the Night of Glass, the Loom broke. Nobody living knows why. Its shards crossed the night sky burning; over a few places they passed low, and there the sky was cut and never healed. The Farshore, a fishing island east of Eastbrook Vale, lies beneath the widest of those scars.

A rift is a nightmare pressing through one of those cuts: a room of the Sleeping World forcing its way into ours, and everything that lives in it, Riftspawn and stalkers and worse, comes spilling through with it. But the creatures are the loud danger. The quiet one is dream-matter itself, because anything it touches that nobody is holding, it slowly rewrites: a coiled rope re-ties itself wrong, a machine forgets its instructions along with its shape. The only thing that can stop it is a living mind, someone present and paying attention, forcing the world to stay itself. Remember that, because the whole story ends on it.

Small rifts blow in under the wounded sky the way squalls blow in off the sea: often, unannounced, and usually gone by morning, and the islanders meet them the same way, with watchfulness rather than panic. But beneath the eastern fields, roughly once a century, the pressure gathers into something no one rides out: the great breach, a wound the size of a battlefield. The hundred-year storm.

Star-glass

The wounded sky sheds. After storms, shards of hard, faintly ringing glass wash up on the Farshore tidelines and nowhere else: star-glass, the broken border's own material, and the only thing a rift answers to. Shaped and tuned, it hears the Sleeping World pressing in before anything opens. Set in a pattern, it anchors a wound shut. Packed and fired, it burns. One material is the island's warning system, its weapon, and its economy: the mainland's wards and beacons are all cut from Farshore glass, and the salvage trade is why a town lives on a wounded island on purpose. The danger is priced in; the living is good.

The Vigil and the rite

After the Night of Glass an order was founded to watch the sky's wounds: the Vigil. Not an army; a profession, closer to lighthouse-keeping than war, with two duties: hear them coming, seal them shut. Its craft is the rite, and the rite is procedure, not sorcery: star-glass wards set around a wound and tuned (verses, counter-notes, timed strokes) until the wound is anchored shut, and an anchored wound knits closed. A small form for the daily rifts; a great form for the century breach: four wards around the rim, tuned in sequence, and nobody goes in. Twelve centuries old, boring, tested, and it works. That matters.

Gullhaven, the daughter-house

Twelve centuries ago the Vigil's mother-house at the Old Beacon sent founders to the Farshore, to keep the watch over fisherfolk who would not leave the richest shoals in the region. They carried one founding gift: the Bellheart, shaped star-glass closed around a splinter of the broken Loom itself, made so the Vigil may hear the night coming. A bell was the safest way to make such a thing speak, so it hung at the heart of a great bell above the harbor.

The Bellheart vibrates before a rift opens: it hears them coming. From that grew the bell code, the island's warning language: one toll, danger in the fields; two tolls, danger at the cliffs; three tolls, the enemy is within the walls. Children learn to count strokes before they learn to read. Somewhere in the centuries the thread home frayed: the Old Beacon forgot its daughter-house, and Gullhaven kept the watch anyway. The Vigil survives here as job titles and a rite-book worn soft. The habit outlived the institution. The habit still works.

The lost heart, and Hale's year

Generations ago the original bell tower collapsed into the tidal vault beneath the Sundered Cliffs and took the Bellheart down with it. The town hung an ordinary iron bell, and it serves: a faint echo of the drowned instrument carries up through the old stonework, enough for the daily code. Nobody alive has needed better.

Then the great breach came due, one hundred years ago, and for the first time since the founding the outer rite was raised without the founders' instrument. It did not take. When the outer rite fails there is one recourse, old as the order: a warden carries the seal inside, to the wound's heart, and raises the ward there. Warden Hale went in with the rite and his two hands, and did not come out. The breach closed. His statue stands above the harbor steps; the plinth carries one name for every warden who ever went inside, with room for more. Why the ones who go in stay, nobody living can say: none of them came back to write it down. And one thing went unreckoned for a century: the failure had a cause. Hale's rite failed because the Bellheart was already at the bottom of the sea. Exactly one person will ever connect those two facts, and he will be right.

The present

The present squad is the best the island has produced: Coalfast, who commands; Ollun, who listens to breaches the way physicians listen to chests; Edda, who builds and names her machines; Saul, who heals people by their names; Tam, who keeps the bell and claims not to believe in the rites he performs perfectly. Six chairs; Outrider Bren died weeks ago on a routine sealing run, so one is empty, and his daughter Nell runs the bell messages because somebody must. And the breach is due. For a year the signs have run wrong: rifts more often, spawn bigger and stranger, counts that fit no breach in the records. There has never been a year like this one.

The Last Bell

A newcomer walks into Gullhaven the day the street stops to count a bell stroke, and proves within hours that they outclass the island's defenses: when a stalker digs into the Tidemill and the militia fold, the newcomer goes in alone and comes out alive. The wardens, walking home from cliff work, walk into the noise. Short a scout in the worst season on record, Coalfast recruits a proven hand.

One night on the rift-line beside him is the night the counts break twelve centuries of records. So Ollun goes after the one instrument that can hear what is coming and power a seal at its scale, and says why in one sentence: the last time this island raised the rite without the Bellheart, it cost Hale. The squad brings the founders' instrument up out of the drowned tower, and it comes up broken: the Loom splinter at its core is whole, but the shaped glass around it cracked in the tower's fall. When Tam strikes it in daylight it gives out a flawed murmur, and every open rift on the island still answers, faintly. Twelve centuries under the sea, and it still hears. It just cannot speak.

The council never debates how to close the breach; everyone knows how. It debates calling the mainland for relief: hands to hold walls and empty the town while the rite is raised. Coalfast argues no: the crossing is exposed, and a signal that loud may be heard by more than the mainland. He loses the vote and carries out the decision as his own. The newcomer votes too, and owns a share of what follows.

The fleet comes; the breach surges hours early; the flare goes up on a defensible call: night water, or ships anchored off a beach nobody holds. The Riftjaw is waiting in the marked channel. The fleet dies in full view, and the creature swallows the sailors' souls, points of light pulled under in rows. The wound feeds.

What is left of the fleet becomes the seal. The squad salvages its wards and instruments for star-glass (they came to hold the line; now they will), Ollun rewrites a rite meant for a house of wardens to fit six pairs of hands, and at Edda's forge the Bellheart is reforged, its cracked voice rebuilt course by course from the dead fleet's glass. When Tam strikes it this time, the note comes out whole, and every open rift on the island answers at full voice. The newcomer helped raise it from the sea and helped rebuild it at the bench. Before the end, they will strike it.

The outer seal is raised by the book, with the Bellheart, and it nearly takes. Then it fails, for a reason no record holds: this wound's heart is not inside the waking world, and nothing anchored outside can hold a wound whose middle is somewhere else. Recognition, not panic: Hale's year. But Ollun contradicts the precedent: Hale had the rite and his two hands; they have the Bellheart and every charge Edda could build. Hold the wound shut from inside. Burn out the heart. Set the scar from the healthy side, the Bellheart struck outside at the instant of fire. Walk in, set it, walk out, all of us. Coalfast, who has read Hale's account more times than anyone alive, says nothing.

Inside, the plan works for ten seconds. Then hands leave the seated Bellheart and the ward drifts, and the squad learns what Hale learned standing where he stood: seated is not held. A living mind must correct the ward until the fire; the one who holds becomes the seal. The old price is back, and Coalfast's hand is already on the collar. One stays.

Then the wound answers. It divides, ward after ward, and it kills the fuse: anything not held by a mind, it rewrites. It has learned the rite, and fed on a fleet, it is strong enough to answer it. Four wards and a trigger that needs a hand: five stations. It counted them. One at a time, without a speech among them, they take their places: Tam, who alone hears the counter-note; Ollun, who alone reads the drifting north; Saul, who holds the holders; Edda, whose charge needs its maker. The last order is one word the newcomer has heard since their first night: positions.

The newcomer is ordered out, and it is not mercy: the scar must be set from the healthy side, and every hand inside has a job only it can do. So does theirs. The charge fires. The strike lands. The breach collapses like a held breath let out; Gullhaven is bought another century. The music stops.

After the bell

The newcomer closes the watch alone: night rounds through an empty redoubt, one end-of-watch duty for each of the five, five names written into the watch log. The iron clapper goes back into the bell, and Nell takes the post: the bell only ever asks one question, who will come, and for twelve hundred years the answer was them, and now it is her. The Bellheart, its voice carried in the dead fleet's glass, travels with Ollun's record to the Old Beacon, where the keepers learn their forgotten daughter-house kept the Vigil for twelve centuries without relief. Set beside the beacon, the heart rings once with no bell around it: the next great breach, anywhere, will be heard coming.

Two threads stay live. Beneath the Wreckfields the wounded Riftjaw still holds the souls it swallowed; killing it frees them into the Dreamer's deep sleep, the one place the nightmares cannot follow. It is justice, not resurrection. And the thing that learned the rite is still on the far side of the scar. That is another story.
